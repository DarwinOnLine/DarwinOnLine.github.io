# When everyone thinks they can do your job

*Or how to keep your eyes open when tools make expertise invisible.*

To earn their licence, London taxi drivers had to pass (and still do) a fearsome exam called
[The Knowledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxis_of_London#Knowledge_of_London). Years of study to memorise
25,000 streets, thousands of landmarks, one-way systems, shortcuts. A monumental body of knowledge, etched into
the brain through sheer repetition. [Neuroscience studies](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.070039597) even showed
that their hippocampus (the brain region linked to spatial memory) was physically larger than average.

And then... GPS happened.

Overnight, anyone with a smartphone could navigate London just as effectively. Or so it seemed. Because GPS
doesn't understand the passenger who says "drop me near the red pub by the station" and means the
*[Old Red Lion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Red_Lion,_Islington)* in Angel, not the
*[Red Lion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Lion,_Westminster)* in Westminster (there are
[dozens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Lion) across Greater London). It doesn't know that this
rushed passenger prefers the slightly longer route with no traffic lights, or that this pedestrian alley is
actually open to taxis at certain hours. That kind of knowledge isn't in any database.
It's in the mind of someone who's done the job, day after day, for years.

But the passenger doesn't know that. Doesn't care, either. For them, the outcome is the same: they arrive.
So why pay more for knowledge that a free app seems to replace?

London cabbies asked themselves that question before pretty much everyone else. Now, pretty much everyone is
asking it.

## The open toolbox

This phenomenon is nothing new. Only the scale has changed.

When the printing press appeared, scribes watched people with zero calligraphy skills produce legible texts at
scale. When calculators entered schools, maths teachers felt something wobble. When digital photography replaced
film, getting a good shot no longer required hours in a darkroom.

Every time, the same pattern: a tool arrives, lowers the barrier to entry, and suddenly untrained people produce
results that *look like* the professional's output. And every time, the same quiet question in the expert's
mind: *am I still needed?*

But until now, it hit one profession at a time. Today, artificial intelligence is hitting (almost) all of them
at once. The photographer watches an amateur retouch photos in a single click. The translator sees DeepL
produce fluent text. The graphic designer watches a novice generate visuals with Midjourney. The musician hears
compositions made by people who can't read sheet music. The developer (oh hey, that's me) watches non-technical
people build working applications without writing a single line of code.

The question is no longer confined to a single profession. It's everywhere.

## Two stings, two realities

Faced with this democratisation, there are two situations that look alike on the surface but couldn't be more
different underneath. Two kinds of encounters that trigger the same feeling in the expert, yet don't mean the
same thing at all.

**The first sting** is when a non-expert teaches you something. The weekend DIYer who shows the carpenter a
trick found on YouTube. The amateur who combined two tools in a way the pro hadn't considered. The carpenter
hasn't become incompetent. The other person hasn't become a carpenter. They simply had an angle the expert
didn't, precisely *because* they're not an expert. No reflexes, no mental ruts, no "we do it this way because
we've always done it this way."

It's an ego sting. Unpleasant, but healthy. And if you set aside your ego for two minutes, it's enriching.

**The second sting** is when a non-expert denies your expertise. Not someone who teaches you, someone who tells
you that what you know is worthless. That the tool does the job, so the skill behind it is obsolete. That's the
person who shows an IKEA shelf to a cabinet-maker and says: "See? No need for your workshop anymore." It's the
traveller with Google Translate who tells a conference interpreter: "Honestly, what more do you do?"

That stings too. But it's not a lesson. It's a misunderstanding.

The problem is that both trigger **the same feeling**. The same doubt, the same "what if they're right?". And
when you don't sort them out, when you put the inventive tinkerer and the ignorant naysayer in the same
emotional basket, you end up doubting everything, including things you know full well to be true.

## Invisible expertise

Here's the cruel paradox of tool democratisation: **the better the tool, the more it makes the expert's work
invisible**.

When DeepL translates a text correctly, you don't see what the interpreter does in a tense negotiation. When an
AI generates a stunning image, you don't see all the decisions an artist makes to create theirs.

  alt="Behind the scenes of The Matrix: technicians in green suits holding the actors, invisible on screen" style="max-width: 100%;" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption>Expertise is the green suit: essential to the result, invisible in the final product.</figcaption>
</figure>

The visible result is the same (or close enough). So the conclusion seems logical: the path to get there no
longer matters.

And let's be honest: **for a good chunk of use cases, that's true**.

For 95% of trips, GPS is enough. For most everyday translations, DeepL gets the job done. For a LinkedIn post
visual, Midjourney does just fine.

Expertise shows up in the remaining 5% (OK, this statistic is the result of a lengthy process called
"the wet finger method" <small>results guaranteed</small>, but you get the idea). It's when the passenger asks
for "a fast route but not a stressful one" and GPS doesn't know what "stressful" means. It's when in a business
negotiation between a Japanese and a German executive, the first one says "it's difficult" meaning "no" and the
second takes it as "let's try harder", and no tool will catch the misunderstanding. It's when the generated
image is gorgeous but follows none of the composition rules an art director would apply instinctively, and the
client can't say why "something feels off" but senses it.

Let's call it **the wall**. The moment the tool is no longer enough. Where the case is borderline, the context
ambiguous, things go sideways. When winter comes.

The wall exists. But let's be clear about two things.

First, **the wall is receding**. Tools are improving, and cases that required expertise yesterday fall within
the tool's reach today. Today's GPS is incomparably better than the one from 2010. DeepL understands context in
ways that would have seemed impossible five years ago.

Second, **the market doesn't pay for the 5%**. Or not enough. The expert cabbie is right on the merits: he's
better than GPS in complex situations. But Uber still won, because the market is sized for the 95% where "good
enough" is the rule. Being right and being economically viable are two different things, and confusing them is a
mistake many experts make to reassure themselves.

And maybe you came here hoping I'd give you a warm hug on this topic, but I've been through it too. It's
uncomfortable to accept. But it's necessary.

## The bias that doesn't pick sides

> The tool will always figure it out

I've had this conversation. Multiple times. With people who sincerely believe the tool has made expertise
obsolete.

When you explain the limitations (maintenance, edge cases,
[hidden debt](/en/blog/the-real-cost-of-ai-when-the-bill-comes-due), the blind spots the tool can't see),
they reply: "The tool will improve." When you mention the resources needed, energy costs, physical constraints,
they say: "We'll find solutions." When you bring up historical precedents where the same promise was made and
never kept, they shrug.

This isn't a debate. It's another wall. The wall of **belief**.

There's a name for it:
the [backfire effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belief_perseverance). Presenting evidence
against a deeply held belief doesn't weaken it — it *reinforces* it. The person doesn't process your argument
as information; they process it as an attack, and dig in further.

A [study by Dan Kahan at Yale](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2319992) showed that people
with the strongest quantitative reasoning skills were *more* polarised, not less: they used their intelligence
to better defend their beliefs, not to question them. It's not stupidity that makes us immune to opposing
arguments — it's the need to protect a worldview we've invested in emotionally.

And now, the uncomfortable part: **this bias applies to us experts too**.

We see it in others easily enough — the friend who refuses to believe their miracle diet has no scientific
basis, the colleague convinced their work methodology is perfect despite three consecutive failures — but we're
blind to it in ourselves. A developer who's invested fifteen years in their expertise feels an enormous
psychological cost in admitting that part of it has been commoditised. A translator who spent years fine-tuning
their ear to linguistic subtleties has every reason in the world to downplay DeepL's progress, even when it's
real.

The backfire effect doesn't pick sides. It hits those who blindly believe in the tool *and* those who refuse to
admit it has changed the game. The former overestimate the tool. The latter overestimate the irreplaceability of
their knowledge. And both use their intelligence to defend their position rather than examine it.

When I catch myself dismissing an AI-produced result with a "yeah but that won't hold up in production", am I
making a clear-eyed assessment, or am I protecting my emotional investment in fifteen years of craft? The honest
answer is: sometimes one, sometimes the other.

And I can't always tell the difference.

## The creeping doubt

There's a real danger in all this, though, and it comes neither from the tool nor from democratisation. It comes
from **silence**.

After enough sterile conversations with people who won't acknowledge facts, you stop talking. And when you stop
talking, a small voice starts whispering: *what if I'm the one who's wrong?*

It's [impostor syndrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impostor_syndrome) in its most vicious form. Not the one
usually described (the junior afraid of not measuring up). No — it's the expert who starts doubting their
expertise, not because they failed, but because nobody recognises their success.

It's a slow poison. Every "you're overcomplicating things" feeds it. Every "the tool does the same thing" feeds
it. Every demonstration of an effortless result (even if nobody checks whether it actually holds up) feeds it.

And the people most affected are often the most competent. Because the more you know, the more you measure what
you don't know. While the person who knows nothing charges ahead with unshakeable confidence (the
[Dunning-Kruger effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dunning%E2%80%93Kruger_effect), once again, always),
you're ruminating over every detail you could have done better.

But be careful not to turn this observation into a comfort blanket. "Competent people doubt, therefore if I
doubt, I must be competent." It's a tempting syllogism, but it's also a logical trap. Doubt is not proof of
competence. It can also be the legitimate signal that something has changed and you need to adapt.

The real question isn't "am I still competent?" but **"is my competence still in the right place?"**

## Validation, legitimacy, and the real world

When someone denies your competence, you want to prove them wrong. And when the other person refuses to see, you
feel like you've failed, as if their understanding were a prerequisite for your legitimacy.

It isn't. A surgeon doesn't need a patient to understand the difficulty of an operation to be a good
surgeon. An airline pilot doesn't need passengers to know what they do during an engine failure to be competent.

But in the real world, there's a nuance that philosophical comfort doesn't cover: **the market's recognition of
your expertise determines your salary, your clients, your career**. The surgeon doesn't need the patient to
understand, sure. But they need the hospital, insurers, and the healthcare system to recognise the difference
between them and a surgical robot. The restaurateur shouldn't doubt themselves because an influencer posts
"super easy 5-minute recipes". But if customers stop coming because "it's all the same", their self-confidence
won't pay the rent or their staff's wages.

Saying "you don't need their validation" is true as a psychological principle, but it's insufficient as a
professional strategy.

The confusion between **the visible result** and **the underlying competence** may not be your problem to solve
intellectually. But its economic consequences are very much your problem.

## What democratisation actually changes

That more people can create, produce, experiment thanks to tools — that's a good thing.
That access to basic know-how is no longer reserved for those with five years of study — that's a good thing.
Democratisation is not the problem.

What's changing isn't the value of expertise. It's **where it shows up**.

Before, expertise was visible from the start. You couldn't produce a result without it. Now, it becomes visible
at the end — when things get complicated, when the tool is no longer enough, when you need to understand *why*
something isn't working and not just *how* to make something work.

It's like the difference between driving on a motorway and driving on black ice. On the motorway, everyone
drives just fine. Black ice is where the driver's expertise makes the difference.

But here's what the scribes of the 15th century can teach us: those who survived the printing press weren't the
ones who "kept at it" hoping someone would recognise the superiority of the quill over the press. They were the
ones who **repositioned**. Some became typographers. Others became luxury calligraphers, going where the machine
couldn't. Others vanished.

Psychological resistance to doubt is necessary. But it's not enough without a **strategy for adaptation**.

Expertise doesn't disappear. It *shifts*. And the most valuable skill may no longer be the one you already have
— it's your ability to recombine it with the new tools rather than *against* them. The cabbie who knows London
by heart *and* uses GPS to optimise travel times in real time is stronger than either one alone. The developer
who understands architecture *and* knows how to steer an AI to speed up implementation produces something
neither could produce separately.

It's not "the tool or you". It's "the tool without you isn't worth much, and you without the tool are leaving
leverage on the table."

## What to do with doubt

I don't have a neat conclusion to hand you. No "5 steps to regain confidence in your expertise" (send "doubt"
and I'll DM you my PDF). Doubt doesn't get resolved — it gets transformed.

A few things that help me, not as a method, but as a compass.

You already know the difference between the sting that teaches you and the sting that dismisses you. The first
deserves your attention, the second doesn't deserve your energy. That sorting is the starting point.

After that, it's the lucidity that costs. If you spend more time explaining why your expertise is irreplaceable
than observing how the ground is shifting beneath your feet, you might be protecting your ego rather than your
career. Adapt: not by giving up what you know, but by finding where it still has the most value, and combining
it with the tools rather than treating them as adversaries. The scribes who became typographers didn't betray
calligraphy. They understood where the world was heading.

And speak up. Not to convince the sceptics. To break the silence with those living through the same thing. The
photographer who doubts, the translator who doubts, the developer who doubts: we're on the same team. Impostor
syndrome thrives in isolation, but talking about it
[wears it down](/en/blog/developer-in-the-age-of-ai-between-vertigo-and-lucidity).

---

The wall is there. It's always been there. But it's receding. And those who don't adapt to its new contour risk
ending up on the wrong side — not because they were wrong, but because they were right too late.

And if you're still doubting: doubt is neither proof of competence nor a sign of obsolescence. It's a signal.
What you do with it is up to you.
