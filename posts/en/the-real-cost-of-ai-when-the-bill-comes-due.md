# The real cost of AI: when the bill comes due

*$200 a month is expensive. But it's probably not enough.*

You pay for your Claude Code, ChatGPT Pro, or GitHub Copilot subscription. Maybe you think it's expensive. Or maybe you think it's reasonable given what it brings you. Either way, there's something you should know: **the price you pay today doesn't reflect the actual cost of the service**. Not even close.

What you use every day is funded by tens of billions of dollars in venture capital, colossal energy infrastructure, and a collective bet that "it'll eventually become profitable." We're in the Uber phase of AI: dirt-cheap rides funded by investors hoping that volume will eventually offset losses.

## What AI actually costs

### Training a model: the nine-figure entry ticket

Training a language model like GPT-4 cost roughly [$78 million](https://epoch.ai/blog/how-much-does-it-cost-to-train-frontier-ai-models) in compute alone. That already sounds massive, but it's almost modest compared to what's coming.

Dario Amodei, Anthropic's CEO, [stated in 2024](https://www.tomshardware.com/tech-industry/artificial-intelligence/ai-models-that-cost-dollar1-billion-to-train-are-in-development-dollar100-billion-models-coming-soon-largest-current-models-take-only-dollar100-million-to-train-anthropic-ceo) that **$1 billion** training runs were already underway, with $10 billion and then $100 billion models to follow within two to three years. According to [Epoch AI](https://epoch.ai/data-insights/cost-trend-large-scale), the cost of training frontier models has been growing **2.4x per year** since 2016.

Do the math. In 2024, we were talking hundreds of millions. By 2027, we'll be talking tens of billions.

For a single model.

### Inference: the silent money pit

But training, as expensive as it is, is a one-time cost. The real money pit is [**inference**](https://cloud.google.com/discover/what-is-ai-inference): every request you send, every line of code the AI generates, every conversation you have. It consumes compute power, continuously, at the scale of millions of simultaneous users.

OpenAI spent [$8.7 billion on inference](https://www.theregister.com/2025/11/12/openai_spending_report/) in the first three quarters of 2025. Just running its models to answer your prompts.

And Sam Altman himself [confirmed in January 2025](https://fortune.com/2025/01/07/sam-altman-openai-chatgpt-pro-subscription-losing-money-tech/) that OpenAI was **losing money on the $200/month Pro plan**, because users consume far more than expected. You read that right: even at $200 a month, the math doesn't work out.

## Who's footing the bill today?

If subscriptions don't cover the costs, who's funding the gap? The answer: venture capital, and a whole lot of faith.

### The numbers that make your head spin

In 2024, OpenAI generated [$3.7 billion in revenue against $5 billion in losses](https://www.cnbc.com/2024/09/27/openai-sees-5-billion-loss-this-year-on-3point7-billion-in-revenue.html). In other words, the company was **spending $2.35 for every dollar earned**.

In 2025, things didn't improve: $13 billion in revenue against [$9 billion in cash burn](https://fortune.com/2025/11/12/openai-cash-burn-rate-annual-losses-2028-profitable-2030-financial-documents/). And projections for 2026 forecast losses tripling to $14 billion.

The cumulative cash burn projected through 2029? [**$115 billion.**](https://www.theinformation.com/articles/openai-boost-revenue-forecasts-predicts-112-billion-cash-burn-2030)

On Anthropic's side, the picture is similar: [$5.6 billion burned in 2024](https://sacra.com/c/anthropic/), a hoped-for break-even in 2028, and fundraising round after fundraising round (the latest, in February 2026, at $30 billion).

### The Uber parallel

Remember Uber's early days. Impossibly cheap rides, funded by billions in venture capital. The goal: conquer the market, crush the competition, then raise prices once the monopoly is locked in.

Today, an Uber ride costs significantly more than it did five years ago. The subsidy phase is over.

AI is in that same subsidy phase. The prices you pay today are artificially low. And when investors want to see a return, the bill will be on you.

### The ghost of the dotcom bubble

The Uber parallel is telling, but the one with the [Internet bubble of 2000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dot-com_bubble) might be even more relevant. Back then, the Nasdaq lost 78% of its value, wiping out roughly $5 trillion in market cap. Telecoms had laid over [80 million miles of fiber optic cable](https://ideas.ted.com/an-eye-opening-look-at-the-dot-com-bubble-of-2000-and-how-it-shapes-our-lives-today/), of which [85 to 95% sat unused](https://archive.seattletimes.com/archive/?date=20020113&slug=darkfiber130) for years.

The similarities are striking. [Sam Altman himself](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/10/21/are-we-in-an-ai-bubble.html) has admitted that an AI bubble is likely. [Ray Dalio](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/10/21/are-we-in-an-ai-bubble.html) (Bridgewater Associates, also the author of *Principles*, a book that elevated the art of stating the obvious to a life philosophy) believes current investment levels are "very similar" to those of the dotcom era. And the share of US GDP dedicated to AI (1.3%, heading toward 1.5 to 2% in 2026) already [exceeds by a third](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/10/21/are-we-in-an-ai-bubble.html) what was dedicated to the internet during the bubble.

But here's the crucial nuance: after the 2000 crash, Amazon went from $107 to $7 per share, before becoming the giant we know today. The "useless" fiber from the 2000s became the backbone of YouTube, Netflix, and cloud computing. **A bubble doesn't mean the technology is dead. It means today's investors risk paying for the infrastructure that others will exploit tomorrow.**

### 95% freeloaders

Here's a telling detail: [95% of ChatGPT users are on the free plan](https://www.theregister.com/2025/10/15/openais_chatgpt_popular_few_pay/). The bulk of usage is funded by a minority of paying subscribers and by mountains of venture capital. This model only holds up as long as investors believe in it.

And when you look at who's investing in whom, the picture gets even more troubling. Microsoft pours billions into OpenAI, and OpenAI spends most of that money on Azure credits... at Microsoft. Google invests in Anthropic, which buys cloud... from Google. Amazon invests in Anthropic too, which uses AWS. It's a nearly closed loop where a handful of giants [pass money back and forth](https://www.wheresyoured.at/is-there-any-real-money-in-renting-out-ai-gpus/) while mutually inflating each other's revenue figures. Big Tech's "AI" revenues are largely other Big Tech's expenses.

## The energy wall

Money is one thing. But behind the dollars, there's a physical reality: AI devours energy at a scale that few people grasp.

### Datacenters that swallow cities

According to the [Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory](https://newscenter.lbl.gov/2025/01/15/berkeley-lab-report-evaluates-increase-in-electricity-demand-from-data-centers/), servers dedicated to AI consumed between 53 and 76 TWh in 2024. The projection for 2028: **165 to 326 TWh**. By 2030, global datacenters could reach [**945 TWh**](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/energy-demand-from-ai), the equivalent of Japan's entire current electricity consumption. The whole country.

Wholesale electricity prices have already [risen 267%](https://www.bloomberg.com/graphics/2025-ai-data-centers-electricity-prices/) near datacenter clusters, according to Bloomberg.

### The water you don't see

A hyperscale datacenter consumes up to [5 million gallons of water per day](https://www.eesi.org/articles/view/data-centers-and-water-consumption) for cooling, the equivalent of a city of 50,000 people. AI's water footprint in 2025 is estimated between [312 and 764 billion liters](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666389925002788).

When you ask ChatGPT to rephrase an email, somewhere on Earth, water is cooling the GPUs that answer you.

And that "somewhere" is often in countries that don't have enough water to begin with. In Latin America, datacenters set up shop where energy is cheap, without much concern for the water they pump. In Chile, in the middle of a [megadrought since 2010](https://earth.org/chile-water-crisis/) ([the worst in 1,000 years](https://www.downtoearth.org.in/climate-change/megadrought-in-chile-both-natural-climate-factors-human-induced-global-warming-to-blame-83947)), Google had its permit [partially revoked](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/world/google-to-pause-plans-for-big-data-center-in-chile-over-water-worries) for its Cerrillos datacenter, which planned to extract **7 billion liters of water per year** from an already stressed aquifer, the equivalent of 80,000 people's consumption. Its first datacenter in Quilicura already pumps [169 liters per second](https://algorithmwatch.org/en/protests-against-data-centers/) from aquifers in a region suffering from persistent droughts.

In Uruguay, a Google project planned to draw [7.6 million liters of drinking water per day](https://www.onegreenplanet.org/environment/uruguayans-protest-googles-plan-use-water-drought/) directly from the public supply, while the government was mixing saltwater into tap water to manage the worst drought in 74 years. In Mexico, Queretaro's datacenters pump from an aquifer already running a [57-billion-liter deficit](https://www.context.news/ai/thirsty-data-centres-spring-up-in-water-poor-mexican-town), while 17 of the region's 18 municipalities are in drought.

The proposed solution? Switch to air cooling. That cuts water usage by 90%, but [increases electricity costs by 10 to 18%](https://www.vertiv.com/en-emea/about/news-and-insights/articles/blog-posts/quantifying-data-center-pue-when-introducing-liquid-cooling/). You don't solve the problem, you just move it.

### The Achilles' heel: chips and rare earths

Behind the energy and water, there's an even more fundamental bottleneck: hardware. [NVIDIA controls 85 to 92%](https://www.nasdaq.com/articles/nvidias-85-gpu-market-share-faces-growing-competition-ai-stock-still-buy-2026) of the AI GPU market. And those GPUs can only be manufactured in essentially one place on Earth: [TSMC, in Taiwan](https://foreignpolicy.com/2026/03/09/artificial-intelligence-ai-sovereignty-taiwan-semiconductor-manufacturing-tsmc-chip-supply-chain-united-states-china/), which produces roughly 90% of the world's most advanced chips.

Taiwan, an island that China claims as its territory. Bloomberg Economics estimates the impact of a conflict at roughly [$10 trillion](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2024-01-09/if-china-invades-taiwan-it-would-cost-world-economy-10-trillion), or 10% of global GDP. TSMC's Arizona plant? It [currently produces 4nm chips](https://www.aztechcouncil.org/tsmc-arizona-fab21-already-making-4nm-chips/), while Taiwan is already moving to 2nm.

And the rare earths needed to manufacture these chips? China [refines 91%](https://www.iea.org/commentaries/with-new-export-controls-on-critical-minerals-supply-concentration-risks-become-reality) of the world's production. In April 2025, Beijing imposed export restrictions on 7 of the 17 rare earth elements, causing a [93% drop in rare earth magnet exports to the United States](https://www.china-briefing.com/news/chinas-rare-earth-export-controls-impacts-on-businesses/).

All [HBM memory production for 2026 is already sold out](https://www.artificialintelligence-news.com/news/ai-chip-shortage-enterprise-ctos-2025/), with 6 to 12-month lead times, at the expense of the automotive and consumer electronics industries.

AI doesn't just depend on money and energy. It depends on a geopolitically fragile supply chain, concentrated in a handful of companies and countries.

### The pharaonic investments

Big Tech isn't messing around. Combined infrastructure spending (capex) from Amazon, Microsoft, Google, and Meta reached [~$380 billion in 2025](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/10/31/tech-ai-google-meta-amazon-microsoft-spend.html), with projections of [over $600 billion for 2026](https://techblog.comsoc.org/2025/12/22/hyperscaler-capex-600-bn-in-2026-a-36-increase-over-2025-while-global-spending-on-cloud-infrastructure-services-skyrockets/).

The Stargate project, announced by OpenAI and SoftBank, plans [$500 billion over four years](https://openai.com/index/announcing-the-stargate-project/) to build datacenters.

Jensen Huang, NVIDIA's CEO, calls AI the ["biggest infrastructure project in the history of humanity"](https://blogs.nvidia.com/blog/davos-wef-blackrock-ceo-larry-fink-jensen-huang/). He's right. The question is who gets the bill.

## The $600 billion question

David Cahn, an analyst at Sequoia Capital (one of the world's largest venture capital firms), posed the uncomfortable question in [a now-seminal article](https://sequoiacap.com/article/ais-600b-question/): to justify current AI investments, the industry would need to generate **$600 billion in annual revenue**. Not by 2035. Now.

His math is simple: take NVIDIA's GPU revenues, multiply by two (to cover the total cost of a datacenter), then multiply by two again (for the margins of the services running on those GPUs). That gives you the minimum revenue the ecosystem needs to produce for the investment to make sense.

We're nowhere close. Ed Zitron, in his newsletter [Where's Your Ed At](https://www.wheresyoured.at/big-tech-2tr/), pushes the math further: for Big Tech to recoup its AI investments by 2030, it would need to generate **$2 trillion in revenue**. Divided among the world's 5 billion internet users, that works out to **$100 per month for every connected person on the planet**. We're not there. He talks about a **["Subprime AI Crisis"](https://www.wheresyoured.at/anthropic-and-openai-have-begun-the-subprime-ai-crisis/)**: thousands of companies integrating generative AI at prices "far from stable and even further from profitable." The subprime parallel is no accident.

Yoshua Bengio, a deep learning pioneer, [warned on France Inter in February 2026](https://www.radiofrance.fr/franceinter/podcasts/l-invite-de-8h20-le-grand-entretien/l-invite-de-8h20-le-grand-entretien-du-jeudi-26-fevrier-2026-4058420) that the $650 billion invested in AI isn't primarily aimed at medicine or climate, but at **automating work**. He cites internal company memos explicitly stating the goal of boosting profitability by cutting headcount.

And the numbers prove him right. Over [276,000 tech workers](https://www.einpresswire.com/article/886940804/new-research-reveals-276-000-tech-workers-lost-jobs-to-ai-driven-layoffs-in-2024-2025) lost their jobs in AI-related layoffs between 2024 and 2025. [Klarna](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/05/14/klarna-ceo-says-ai-helped-company-shrink-workforce-by-40percent.html) went from 5,500 to 3,400 employees, with an AI assistant doing the work of 700 people. [Salesforce](https://www.foxbusiness.com/economy/salesforce-cuts-4000-jobs-due-ai-ceo-says) cut its customer support from 9,000 to 5,000 people, with AI agents handling 50% of interactions.

The most troubling part is that according to the [Harvard Business Review](https://hbr.org/2026/01/companies-are-laying-off-workers-because-of-ais-potential-not-its-performance), companies are laying off workers based on AI's **potential**, not its actual performance. And [56% of CEOs](https://www.pwc.com/gx/en/news-room/press-releases/2026/pwc-2026-global-ceo-survey.html) report **zero benefits** from their AI investments (no savings, no additional revenue). They're cutting jobs based on a promise, not results.

This is where the ROI math changes: if AI can replace an $80,000/year employee with a $20,000/year subscription, the billions invested in infrastructure are justified differently. Not by the $20/month subscription you pay, but by the salaries companies will no longer have to pay.

## What about tomorrow? The honest counterarguments

It would be dishonest not to mention what could change the game. Because there are reasons to hope (or at least to add nuance).

### DeepSeek: the pebble in the shoe

In January 2025, DeepSeek sent shockwaves through Silicon Valley. Its R1 model, trained for [a fraction of the cost](https://www.theregister.com/2025/09/19/deepseek_cost_train/) of American models (roughly $6 million by realistic estimates, versus hundreds of millions for GPT-4), triggered a [$589 billion drop](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/nvidia-stock-plummets-loses-record-589-billion-as-deepseek-prompts-questions-over-ai-spending-135105824.html) in NVIDIA's market cap in a single day, January 27, 2025, the largest market cap loss in US stock market history.

Its API is [20 to 50 times cheaper](https://intuitionlabs.ai/articles/deepseek-inference-cost-explained) than OpenAI's depending on the models compared, with [benchmarks comparable to GPT-4](https://docsbot.ai/models/compare/gpt-4/deepseek-r1) or even better in math and code. All with caveats (Chinese government censorship, fragile infrastructure, [over 74 outages](https://statusgator.com/services/deepseek) in one year), but the proof is there: optimization can drastically cut costs.

### Open source as an escape hatch?

And then there's open source. Meta's Llama (Scout, Maverick, Behemoth) has surpassed [one billion downloads](https://about.fb.com/news/2025/03/celebrating-1-billion-downloads-llama/). Mistral, on the European side, offers competitive models backed by an [1.7 billion euro raise](https://mistral.ai/news/mistral-ai-raises-1-7-b-to-accelerate-technological-progress-with-ai) to hold its own against the American giants.

The most interesting part: these models run locally. A Mac M4 Max with 128 GB of RAM runs a 70B model smoothly. On the PC side, an RTX 5090 with 32 GB of VRAM handles substantial models. Tools like [Ollama](https://ollama.com/) or [llama.cpp](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp) make local deployment accessible to any developer.

The performance gap with frontier models is shrinking: on the MMLU benchmark, there's [only a few points gap](https://whatllm.org/blog/open-source-vs-proprietary-llms-2025) between the best open-source models and proprietary ones. Frontier models still hold the edge on the most complex reasoning tasks, but for everyday use cases (code generation, summarization, translation, writing), open source has become a credible alternative.

This is the most optimistic scenario: a world where AI doesn't depend on a monthly subscription, but runs on your own machine.

### Falling inference prices

Sam Altman [claims](https://blog.samaltman.com/three-observations) that the cost of using a given level of AI drops by a factor of 10 every 12 months. You can question the objectivity of OpenAI's CEO on this point, but the numbers partially back him up: GPT-4o is [83% cheaper on output](https://openai.com/index/gpt-4o-mini-advancing-cost-efficient-intelligence/) than GPT-4 was 16 months earlier. Reaching GPT-3.5's level has become [280 times cheaper](https://www.tomshardware.com/tech-industry/artificial-intelligence/ai-costs-drop-280-fold-but-harmful-incidents-rise-56-percent-in-last-year-stanford-2025-ai-report-highlights-china-us-competition) between 2022 and 2024.

### World models: think less to understand more?

What if the problem isn't optimizing LLMs, but LLMs themselves?

That's the thesis of Yann LeCun, Turing Award winner and deep learning pioneer, who just launched [AMI Labs](https://www.maddyness.com/2026/03/10/yann-lecun-lance-ami-labs-et-leve-plus-d1-milliard-de-dollars/) with a raise of over a billion dollars. His argument: autoregressive LLMs, which predict the next word token by token, are fundamentally wasteful. They simulate understanding by brute-forcing billions of text parameters, without ever grasping the causal structure of the world.

The alternative? "World models," based on the [JEPA](https://ai.meta.com/blog/yann-lecun-ai-model-i-jepa/) (Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture) framework. Instead of generating text word by word, these models build **abstract representations of reality** from videos, 3D environments, and spatial data, somewhat like a human mental model. Predicting in a compact space rather than in the raw data space.

And the early results are promising: [V-JEPA](https://ai.meta.com/blog/v-jepa-yann-lecun-ai-model-video-joint-embedding-predictive-architecture/) trains with **1.5 to 6 times less compute** than comparable vision methods. [VL-JEPA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.10942) achieves similar performance with **50% fewer trainable parameters**. And [Semantic Tube Prediction](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22617) (February 2026) reaches the same accuracy as an LLM with **16 times less training data**, defying the scaling laws we thought were set in stone.

Tempting. But let's add nuance. These gains have mainly been demonstrated in **vision** (images, video), not yet in language at scale. No standardized energy benchmark directly compares a world model and an LLM on the same tasks. AMI Labs hasn't published any consumption data. And the irony isn't lost on anyone: to fund this vision of "leaner" AI, they had to raise a billion dollars. Google DeepMind, OpenAI, and [World Labs](https://www.worldlabs.ai/) are positioning themselves in the same space, which looks more like a new arms race than a computational degrowth scenario.

World models could change AI's energy equation. But for now, it's another billion-dollar promise, not an established fact.

### But...

Optimizations, open source, new architectures: all of it is real, and all of it helps. But these gains are partly offset by the **explosive growth in usage**. We pay less per request, but we make far more of them. Models become more efficient, but we keep asking more of them.

Ilya Sutskever, co-founder of OpenAI, [warned at NeurIPS 2024](https://dlyog.com/papers/one_internet_v1): data is the fossil fuel of AI, and we only have one Internet. Pre-training as we know it will hit its limits. And [several investors and researchers](https://techcrunch.com/2024/11/20/ai-scaling-laws-are-showing-diminishing-returns-forcing-ai-labs-to-change-course/) are already observing diminishing returns in scaling laws.

The result: we're spending exponentially more for increasingly marginal gains.

## What this means for us, developers

OK, the numbers are staggering. But concretely, what does this mean for you and me?

### Accessible AI, but for how long?

Today, you can code with an AI assistant for $20/month. It's democratic, almost too good to be true. But if prices need to rise to reflect actual costs, the landscape changes:

- **AI as a premium tool**: accessible to companies that can afford it, less so to freelancers and small teams.
- **The return of asymmetry**: big companies with infrastructure budgets will get access to the best models; everyone else will make do with degraded or open-source versions.
- **The "AI will do everything for you" mirage**: if every request costs significantly more, you'll think twice before asking AI to refactor your entire codebase.

### The emerging oligopoly

Three players (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google) hold [88% of the enterprise LLM market](https://menlovc.com/perspective/2025-the-state-of-generative-ai-in-the-enterprise/). And when you build your product around an API, you're tying yourself to a provider that can change its prices, terms of service, or deprecate your favorite model overnight.

It's already happened. OpenAI [restricted its terms of use](https://community.openai.com/t/new-openai-services-agreement-effective-may-31-2025/1249405) in May 2025, breaking hundreds of CustomGPTs in the process. And [45% of companies](https://www.swfte.com/blog/avoid-ai-vendor-lock-in-enterprise-guide) say vendor lock-in is already holding back their ability to adopt better tools.

The [Stigler Center](https://www.promarket.org/2025/11/21/preventing-ai-oligopoly-and-digital-enclosure-via-compulsory-access/) (University of Chicago) warns: an unregulated AI oligopoly has undesirable economic, security, and political consequences. The FTC is [investigating](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/01/ftc-issues-staff-report-ai-partnerships-investments-study) the Microsoft-OpenAI and Google-Anthropic partnerships, but antitrust law moves far more slowly than the industry.

### Don't put all your eggs in one basket

The takeaway isn't "AI sucks, don't touch it." It's: **build your skills so you don't depend on a single tool whose price and availability you don't control**.

AI is a tremendous lever. But a lever you rent, not one you own. And the landlord can change the terms of the lease whenever they want. Diversify your providers, explore open source, and never build your value proposition on an API you don't control.

## TL;DR: nothing is free

I'm not telling you AI is going to collapse or that it's a scam. I use these tools daily, and they make me objectively more productive.

But I am telling you that the price you pay today is a teaser rate. That behind every prompt, there's an infrastructure that costs billions and isn't paying for itself yet. That the investors funding all of this aren't running a charity. And that when the subsidy phase ends, someone will have to pay.

That someone will probably be you. In the form of pricier subscriptions, stripped-down free plans, more aggressive usage-based pricing, or enterprise-only access.

The real cost of AI isn't a secret. It's just deferred. And like any debt, the longer you wait, the more the interest piles up.

The only question left: when the bill comes due, will you be ready?
