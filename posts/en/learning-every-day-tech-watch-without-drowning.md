# Learning every day: tech watch without drowning

*Because in this job, what you know today will be obsolete tomorrow.*

Every profession requires staying up to date. A doctor follows research advances. A lawyer keeps track of evolving laws and case law. A plumber, an electrician, a baker, each sees their trade evolve and must adapt to new standards, new practices, new ways of doing things. Few professions allow you to stop learning after your degree.

Software development is no exception, but it has a peculiarity: speed. A popular framework today will be "legacy" in three years, at least in its current form. A widely accepted best practice will become an antipattern when a new paradigm emerges. The language you master will see its syntax evolve, its tools change, its community split over the right way to do things. And meanwhile, new languages, new tools, new ways of thinking appear. Every single day.

This pace is unique to our industry. And if you don't adapt, you don't become bad overnight. You slowly, silently, fall out of step.

Tech watch is what prevents that drift. Not an extra chore, not a luxury for enthusiasts with nothing else to do in the evening: a professional survival tool.

## Why stay informed (beyond the obvious)

"So you don't fall behind", yes. But it's more subtle than that.

**To avoid reinventing the wheel.** The number of times I've seen someone code a homemade solution for a problem that a library already solved, better, in three lines. Not out of incompetence, out of ignorance. You can't use a tool you don't know exists. Tech watch broadens your field of vision so that when a problem arises, you have more solutions in your toolbox.

**To anticipate shifts.** When the JavaScript ecosystem moved from jQuery to Angular to React, those who followed the news saw the transition coming. The others found themselves with skills that no longer matched job listings. Same thing today with AI: those who understand what LLMs do and how to integrate them have a head start over those waiting for things to "stabilize".

**To have an informed opinion.** In meetings, when someone proposes adopting a new technology, you want to be the one who says "I've read experience reports on this, here are the pros and cons" rather than the one who shrugs. Tech watch gives you a legitimate voice in technical decisions.

**To understand, not necessarily to master.** An important point: doing tech watch doesn't mean learning every new technology in depth. The goal is to know that something exists and in what context to use it. When you need it, you'll know where to dig deeper. Nobody can master everything, and the best developers I know don't claim otherwise. They simply know where to look.

**To feed your curiosity.** Let's be honest: if you became a developer, it's probably because you like understanding how things work. Tech watch is fuel for that curiosity. It's stumbling upon an article that explains a concept you'd never encountered and feeling that little rush of "oh, I didn't know that was possible".

## My sources: what works for me

I'm not going to give you a list of 47 newsletters to follow. You won't read them, and neither will I. Here's what I *actually* use, every day.

### Le Journal du Hacker

[Le Journal du Hacker](https://www.journalduhacker.net/) is a French-speaking community aggregator, in the spirit of Hacker News. Articles are submitted and voted on by the community, which creates a fairly effective natural filter. The advantage: it's in French, it's varied (dev, sysadmin, security, open source), and the discussion quality in comments is generally good. It's my daily entry point.

### Human Coders News

[Human Coders News](https://news.humancoders.com/) is another French-speaking community aggregator, focused on development. Articles are submitted and voted on by the community, with a good balance between news, tutorials, and experience reports. If you could only follow two French aggregators, Le Journal du Hacker and Human Coders News would make a great pair.

### Developpez.com

[Developpez.com](https://www.developpez.com/) is the veteran. The site has been around since 1999, and it has a rare virtue: in-depth articles, often long, often technical, often well-sourced. It's not buzz, it's not "top 10 things to know in 2026". It's solid content, with tutorials, courses, FAQs. The design hasn't always kept up with trends, but the content has.

### Blogs via RSS feeds

And then there are blogs. Individual developer blogs where people share their experience reports, their discoveries, their struggles. That's often where you find the most authentic, least formatted, most useful content.

To follow them, I use **RSS feeds**. If you're not familiar, the principle is simple: instead of manually checking each site for updates, you subscribe to its RSS feed, and a reader (Feedly, Miniflux, FreshRSS, or even Thunderbird) centralizes everything for you. It's like a mailbox for websites.

The big advantage of RSS over social media or newsletters: **you choose your sources**. No algorithm deciding what you should read. No sponsored content. No noise. Just the articles from the people you chose to follow, in chronological order.

By the way, this blog has its own RSS feed ([here](/en/feed.xml)). If you like what you're reading, you know what to do. 😉

## Other sources to explore

Beyond my essentials, here are other avenues I browse regularly or recommend.

**[Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/)**, the reference English-speaking aggregator. The source of much of what ends up on Le Journal du Hacker, but earlier and with a broader scope (startups, science, tech in general). The comment quality is often excellent, sometimes brutal.

**[TLDR Newsletter](https://tldr.tech/)**, a daily email summary. Five minutes of reading in the morning, and you have the headlines of what happened in tech. It's surface-level watch, but effective for not missing anything major.

**[Dev.to](https://dev.to/)**, a community blog where anyone can publish. Quality varies (that's the game), but gems exist. A good way to discover different perspectives and concrete experience reports.

**[Reddit r/programming](https://www.reddit.com/r/programming/)**, for those who like the discussion format. Shared links are often interesting, but it's mostly in the comments where things happen: debates, nuances, contradictory experience reports. You have to tolerate the noise to find the signal, but when you find it, it's worth the detour.

**Conference replays** ([Devoxx France](https://www.youtube.com/@DevoxxFRvideos), [MiXiT](https://www.youtube.com/@mixitconf), [dotConferences](https://www.youtube.com/@dotconferences), [Volcamp](https://www.youtube.com/@volcamp8744)): many talks are available for free on YouTube after the event. It's "passive" watch: you play a talk in the background while doing something else, and you absorb. Ideal for days when you don't feel like reading.

**Tech podcasts**, for those who prefer audio. In French, *Artisan Développeur* is a good pick. In English, *Syntax* or *The Changelog* cover the web and open source ecosystem well. But to be perfectly honest, I don't consume these channels myself.

## How not to drown

Because that's the real issue. Sources aren't lacking. What's lacking is a method to keep tech watch from becoming a source of anxiety.

### The 15-minute rule

My daily watch is 15 to 20 minutes in the morning, with a coffee. I scan my RSS feeds, skim the headlines, read the articles that catch my eye, set aside those that deserve a deeper read. And that's it. I don't spend my day refreshing Hacker News.

Some days I read three articles in depth. Other days I just skim the headlines. Both are useful. Skimming gives you the radar, deep reading gives you the understanding.

### Filter without guilt

You won't read everything. That's not only normal, it's desirable. A title and first paragraph are often enough to know if an article will bring you something. The rest, you skip without remorse.

The classic mistake is saving everything "for later" without ever coming back. If you use a tool like Pocket or Wallabag, clean it up regularly. A reading list of 300 unread articles isn't tech watch anymore, it's organized guilt.

### Share to retain

An article you read and forget served no purpose. An article you summarize to a colleague, post in a Slack channel, or mention in a meeting, that one, you remember. Sharing is anchoring.

And if you want to go further: write. A blog post, a note, even a simple thread. Reformulating what you've learned forces you to truly understand it. That's the next level of tech watch, where you go from consumer to contributor.

### Alternate depth and breadth

Every day, you do surface-level watch: headlines, summaries, trends. That's the radar. From time to time, you dig deep into a topic: you read the docs, test a tool, follow a tutorial, build a small side project. That's the sonar.

The two are complementary. The radar tells you "this thing exists". The sonar tells you "this is how this thing works". Don't only do radar (you stay superficial), don't only do sonar (you miss everything else).

## Tech watch as professional hygiene

A doctor reads medical journals. A lawyer follows evolving case law. An accountant stays current on tax changes. Nobody finds that strange. It's professional hygiene.

For a developer, it's the same. Tech watch isn't a side activity you do "when you have time". It's a component of the job. Not to know everything (that's impossible and not the point), but to know that things exist. To have the map, even if you haven't explored every territory.

The day you need a distributed caching solution, or a monitoring tool, or a validation library, you might not know which one to choose immediately. But you'll know they exist, you'll know where to look, and you might even have read an experience report that saves you from a bad choice.

That's what tech watch is. Not an encyclopedia in your head. A radar turned on.

Find your sources, set your pace, accept that you won't read everything. And if you're looking for a starting point, you're already in the right place. [Subscribe to this blog's RSS feed](/en/feed.xml) and start your list.

The hardest part isn't starting. It's not trying to absorb everything at once. Tech watch is a marathon, not a sprint. Fifteen minutes a day beats three hours on Sunday evening in a burst of guilt.

And if one day you stumble upon an article that makes you think "oh, I didn't know that was possible", congratulate yourself. That's exactly why you do tech watch.
