AI versus the State, and it just played out this weekend.

## 2018: when Google backed down

In April 2017, the Pentagon launches Maven (official name: Algorithmic Warfare Cross-Functional Team). The goal: use AI
to automatically analyze video captured by military drones, in order to detect and classify objects, vehicles, people.
Thousands of hours of content no human analyst could ever process by hand.

Google is at the helm, providing its AI expertise (TensorFlow at the time) on a modest contract (a few million dollars,
with the promise of fast growth).

Except... in spring 2018, the affair leaks internally. Several thousand Google employees aren't exactly thrilled to be
working, without knowing it, on this kind of project, and they make it crystal clear to management in an open letter
that basically says: "We're happy to work for you, but not to wage war." Some even resign.

The stake is above all a moral one: it's never said explicitly, but clearly their AI, by "just" analyzing images, feeds
a chain that can end in strikes. The line between intelligence and lethal targeting is blurry, and it's precisely that
blur that bothers them.

In the end, in June 2018, Google announces it won't renew the contract (which ended in 2019) and publishes its AI
Principles, with a commitment not to design AI for weapons.

## Maven isn't dead (neither are the crypto wars)

Maven isn't dead though, it just changed suppliers: it was then picked up by Palantir (the Maven Smart System) and the
Anduril ecosystem. The US didn't give up on its objectives, it just did without Google. The lesson the security
establishment drew from it: "tech companies defect on principle, it's a pain, we need to guard against it."

Back in the 90s we already had the crypto wars, where encryption (civilian-born though it was) was flat-out classified
as a "munition" under export controls, and we got the SAME circus.

## 2026: the Pentagon blacklists Anthropic

Annnnd so, here we are in 2026. We find Palantir, which already had to rip Claude out of its defense systems, not
because Anthropic was pulling out of the military (Claude was already running in the famous Maven Smart System), but
because Anthropic, having refused the unconditional use of its models, had just been blacklisted by the Pentagon. Then
on June 12, the US government purely and simply ordered Anthropic to suspend access to its Fable 5 and Mythos 5 models,
citing "national security" concerns.

## Mechanisms we already know

We find the familiar mechanisms:

Two levers, at two moments. In March, the blacklist for refusing. In June, the suspension for speaking up. Same target,
two pretexts.

**Step 1: the "transparency tax."** Anthropic publishes the limits of its own safeguards. They say "Look, Fable and
Mythos, you can actually divert them like this," that's nice of them but they served the Trump administration the
pretext on a silver platter to hit them with the mother of all stop orders. They documented the dangers, and they
supplied the motives. Confession made, not forgiven at all. The competitor, on the other hand (hi there, OpenAI),
documented nothing of the sort: it just signed, fast, and accepted the terms. No confession, no handle to grab.

**Step 2: the US government showing its hand by being biased**, when the firms are aligned on their red lines: on
military use, Anthropic and OpenAI always say the same thing: refusal of domestic mass surveillance so as not to become
caricatures worthy of Black Mirror, and lethal autonomy (always hard to own that the same kind of AI you use to generate
collectible-figurine photos of yourself also decides to kill people). Yet only one of the two was designated a "national
security risk" and shut out of public markets. So if, for the same principles, the treatment diverges, then the problem
lies elsewhere, and probably on the side of political power dynamics.

Cherry on top: DeepSeek, the AI rubber-stamped by the Chinese Communist Party, never got the label. So we hit an
American company harder than an AI from Beijing. "National security," they tell us.

## So, who sets the limits?

So, who sets the limits? Can a private company impose a moral veto on what a sovereign state wants to do in matters of
defense? On the red lines mentioned, it looks virtuous, but it becomes a lot less so when you imagine other vetoes and
other decision-makers.

The real fault line in the sector has never been technical, it rests instead on a simple question: how much sovereignty
do we concede to the State?
