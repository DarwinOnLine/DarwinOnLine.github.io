# The comedy of mastery

*Don't mistake the craft for its theatre.*

You've seen it before, that LinkedIn post. The one that basically says: I shipped my SaaS in a weekend thanks to Claude.
Screenshot of Stripe showing €200 in monthly revenue, a 🚀 emoji, and the killer line: "no-code, pure prompt, just vibes."

He explains he didn't write a single line of code. That he prompted, designed his product, deployed it.
That AI does everything, you just need to "ask the right questions." Dozens of likes,
plenty of admiring comments, three consulting firms in his DMs asking to meet.

And yet you bump into the author every day. Sometimes he's a PM, sometimes a marketer, sometimes a designer.
Sometimes even a dev. The face changes, the script holds.
He gives off total mastery. But you know. You work with him, or with people like him, and you know he doesn't understand half of what he's handling.
That he has no idea what an index on his database is. That his "scalability" rests on a free Vercel quota and three users a day.
That the day a real client calls, he'll have to pay someone like you to fix what Claude dropped there.

This isn't a critique of him. He played the game he was taught to play. He published at the right time, on the right network, in the right tone.
He turned a demo into a story, and a story into proof. That's exactly what's expected of him in 2026.

It's even what's expected of you, and you know it.

The strange part is, he doesn't look like he's faking. He looks like he believes what he's saying. He might be right to believe it.
Maybe all of this has become true. Maybe the old criteria (knowing how it works, debugging in prod at 3 a.m., explaining your stack to a junior)
have just stopped mattering.

And if that's the case, we have a problem. Not them. Us.

## Us? What do you mean?

Yes, us. You, me, all those who are asked to stay current, always on the front line, whatever the technology.
Us, the ones paid to know, and who agreed to play a game where knowing is no longer enough. Where you have to know,
**AND** show it, **AND** anticipate what's next, **AND** climb the ladder, **AND** look relaxed through all of it.
It's like an arms race, except the weapons are called Claude Opus 52 or Node 99, and nobody bothers asking if you can still read the manual.

I'm talking about that constant injunction to stay current, where you're told "if you're not on top of the latest version of that tool,
you're a *has been*." Where simply taking a week of vacation becomes a backlog to catch up on when you return.

I'm talking about that need to constantly communicate. Not just work, communicate. Say you tested this framework,
read that paper, used that model, and most of all what you thought about it. An opinion. Always an opinion. If you don't have one, you're not following,
and if you're not following, you're out of the game.

I'm talking about that speech you hear too often: "Wait, you've got X years of experience and you're not a manager?
You're not making [insert a salary I've never touched in my life] a month?".
As if expertise was only ever a stepping stone, never a destination.

I'm talking about that constant expectation of the next wave. Like in chess, where you're expected to play at a serious level,
never that of the Sunday hobbyist, even though most people don't calculate three moves ahead.
You have to be on top of things, have an opinion on the next innovation discovered during your tech watch, have tested it yesterday evening
between two episodes of the show everyone else is also watching.

And above all, what magicians call in [The Prestige](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Prestige_(film))
the moment of magic: the instant when the illusion lands and the audience cries "WOW!", the moment when you have to display all of it at once, without showing the seams,
because showing the back of the curtain is an amateur's confession. And being an amateur in your job, like in magic, is a no-go.

The problem is that all of this happens in parallel. Learning Claude 4.7, posting about your learning, becoming a manager,
anticipating Claude 4.8, and giving the impression that it all flows naturally. It's mechanically impossible.
Nobody has the energy for it. So either you crack, or you play the comedy.

And what I'm describing is the lived experience of tech, but if you're not in tech, ask a teacher, a doctor, a lawyer:
they'll tell you the same pile-up with a different vocabulary.


## A comedy in six acts

### Act 1: The post that breaks through

The CTO of the moment just dropped a thread on X: he's telling you about Symfony 99, he details all the new features across 12 tweets,
the possibilities, why we now use Forms like this, etc. In short: it's a game-changer, and it breaks through:
thousands of impressions, of likes, plenty of shares and follows.
But Symfony 99 dropped in beta the day before yesterday. You know he couldn't have tested it seriously, he knows it too,
but that's not the point: others will talk about it too, but he'll have talked about it **FIRST**.

### Act 2: The hidden economy of posture

The CTO may be a little hasty, but mostly he wanted to share early and be noticed.
Now let's talk about the prompt influencer. He comes to explain that thanks to a few prompts, he automated his entire life,
and yours too. By the way, he gives you "the 5 prompts that will change your life."

And right at the end, he offers his €99 course on how to succeed like him.

Because that's what we're talking about: a market where the influencer sells to those who fear falling behind. Impostor syndrome
isn't really a problem anymore, it has become a solvable demand, a product to sell.

It's [The Emperor's New Clothes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Emperor%27s_New_Clothes),
they tell you they're dressing you in the finest garments, but really, you're naked.

### Act 3: AI as a catalyst

Yes, this comedy existed before AI arrived, but now it's nearly universal.
Before, faking knowledge required endurance: vocabulary, references, posture in meetings. You had to keep up the act,
which filtered out the actors. Today, anyone can open an AI assistant, paraphrase, and pass for someone who masters a subject
they're discovering. These are [the Sunday experts](/en/developer-in-the-age-of-ai-between-vertigo-and-lucidity#sunday-experts).

The entry barrier for fake knowledge has collapsed. Nobody can tell the difference anymore between someone who dug for years
and someone who asked three questions the day before.

### Act 4: Does it only concern devs?

No gatekeeping here: does your job involve some form of expertise? Then you're concerned.
You can be a doctor, a teacher, a lawyer, and have a colleague or peer explain something to you that they haven't actually read,
or don't fully master, while you do.

The doctor who cites a meta-analysis they haven't read.

The lawyer who invokes a precedent discovered in the waiting room.

And worst of all: [the person outside your professional circle who can do it too](/en/when-everyone-thinks-they-can-do-your-job).

Tech didn't invent this comedy. It just now has an invisible prompter that can feed any line,
and that prompter is Claude, or any consumer-grade AI agent.

### Act 5: What we actually reward

The problem with his post isn't the topic itself, it's the way it's done, it's symptomatic.
Just like in other professions, the set and the actors change, but the comedy stays the same: we reward
performance in a field rather than its apprenticeship.

The influencer, the layman, and of course the colleague from the opening who shipped his SaaS in a weekend: all of them are the purest face
of this comedy, our modern Sganarelles. They aren't pretending. They've just learned to no longer make
the difference between doing and performing. They're in a system that rewards them for it.

### Act 6: You're not a spectator

And there it is. Six acts, and the curtain doesn't fall. The play loops, and we've ended up forgetting that we were performing.
At this stage, you have to admit you're not just a spectator. You know the codes. You know when to
smile at the right moment, nod in meetings, pretend that yes, you also read the paper.
The comedy has become the norm. If you refuse it, then you have to accept the price.

## The cost for those who refuse

Refusing the comedy has a price. It's not dramatic, you won't cry, but let's talk about it, since I know it well.

First, you collect a few labels: _not ambitious_, _he's stagnating_, _not lead material_.
You see promotions go by that could have been yours, friends becoming leads, others giving conference talks.
And honestly, with no sarcasm whatsoever, I'm happy for them. It's just not my thing.

Then there's the material ceiling. Fifteen years in the job, and I earn less than most of my classmates from school.
I know this because we sometimes talk about it among ourselves, it's not a taboo. It's not because my companies pay badly.
It's that I never wanted to pay the price you had to pay to earn more.
The price was becoming someone else.

So let me be honest, because at this stage, you might be thinking "Okay, he's trying to play the victim" and you'd be right.

I don't only have good reasons to refuse. I love my current working conditions. I love working in a small structure,
I love the rhythm, I've always preferred doing things well over selling them well, I've always preferred to evolve
in the shadows. This refusal is partly a choice... and partly a convenience.
But mostly, I think, a fear: that of having to pretend every day, of not being able to keep up the illusion,
and of losing, through it all, the very essence of my craft.

That's what refusal costs me, and that's what it brings me. You'll obviously find pieces of yourself in this, or nothing at all.
I'm just saying: if you recognise yourself, we're together, and if not, well, don't take me for a model.

This posture has its own blind spots. Let's look at them.


## The posture and its limits

Let's be clear. Having this posture is fine, but it has its limits. You can stop me and say

> Yeah okay, you're telling me you don't need ambition, but that's a bit easy, you have a privileged status
> that makes it comfortable.

And... well, let me explain.

### Ambition isn't a bad thing

When I talk about ambition, I'm talking about my situation as a tech professional who refuses the constant injunction of "you must evolve",
where it's tacitly forbidden to want to excel at your current level, because that level is supposedly reserved for
people less experienced than you.

Ambition is a lever for emancipation for many people. It's even a way out for working-class people
and minorities. Just saying "don't have ambition" is a statement coming from people already settled.

In other words: you have a dream? It feels unreachable but you've got the drive? Then go for it, really.

### Refusing is still a luxury

I said it, refusing to play the comedy has a price, and it's even a luxury. Mine rests on a few things: a permanent contract,
fifteen years of transferable skills, stable material conditions, no precarity at my back. So many
safety nets that let me say no without losing everything. The same refusal, voiced by someone who doesn't have those
comforts, doesn't carry the same weight, and may not even be allowed to be voiced. To act as if this asymmetry didn't exist
would be exactly the comedy of mastery applied to my own position: pretending not to know where I'm
speaking from, and looking the other way.

### Fear of failure as a philosophical posture

I said it earlier: there's a part of fear in my refusal. I don't want to have to pretend every day,
because I know I wouldn't always be able to hold the illusion. I can't always tell what's lucidity and what's
rationalisation. When you disagree with a system, you'd like to believe it's an ethic, when sometimes
it's just self-protection.

I'll probably never know which of the two is more true in me, so I want to be honest with myself and with my limits.

### Work / life balance

Probably not going to make any friends here, but oh well.

There's a pattern that feels to me like the same comedy in a different costume. I observe, in my circle, men of
my generation claiming to share parenting duties 50/50, posting their outings with their kids and their
reflections on modern fatherhood. I don't know what their partners experience, I'm not in their place and I
won't pretend to be. I'm talking about the discourse, because that's what you can hear and observe, and because that
discourse is sometimes mine too. I'm part of it. And that discourse is a story of presence and balance, that sounds right
only if you don't dig.

The same guy who pretends to master a tool he just discovered pretends to share a load he barely skims.

But refusing isn't enough, otherwise it's just vetoing and giving up. And that's where you need to know what you say yes to.


## Beyond refusal

What does "developing" mean? In the classic exercise of "explain your job to me like I'm five", I say:

> I produce simple solutions to problems that are complicated.

Because that's what my job is. Bringing a solution to a problem, solving issues, thinking about how to deploy it,
maintaining it, ensuring a good level of availability, and making sure the original need is met.

Since I started working, this craft has evolved. I've learned to use the tools at my disposal, I've picked up the
good practices, I keep doing my tech watch. I try new things.
But one thing I've learned: when something works, and works well, you keep it.
Not out of fear of change, but to guarantee stability. If the need evolves and the solution no longer
fits, then we see how to improve it and possibly change the tool.

I want to dig and deepen my knowledge of a practice, of a framework, of a technology, so I can think about it
on my own, without systematically asking Claude. I like knowing my solution so I can account for it when a
question or a problem arises, not having a superficial knowledge of it.

Collectively, we've chosen to value the manager over the expert. Yet there's nothing automatic about that, and it
isn't a fatality. Some companies, including the most powerful ones, have
[ladders](https://www.levels.fyi/?compare=Meta,Apple,Google) where a senior technical expert has the same status
as a manager. On the French side, apart from a few companies that imported the model, we talk about "technical expert"
whose ceiling sits lower than the "manager". We're squarely in the symptom I'm describing.

AI, once again, is just a tool among others. A super tool that lets me move faster on tasks
I master but that would have taken me hours. There, yes, I can take on the role of a conductor, because that's what I
do — I direct my AI like I would direct a small team of ~~musicians~~ devs.

---

Refuse to chase glory and do my craft well. Reality isn't a LinkedIn post.
