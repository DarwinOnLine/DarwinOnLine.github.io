# The comedy of mastery

*Don't mistake the craft for its theatre.*

You've seen it before, that LinkedIn post, the one that basically says: I shipped my SaaS in a weekend thanks to Claude.
Screenshot of Stripe showing €200 in monthly revenue, a 🚀 emoji, and the promise "no-code, pure prompt, just vibes."

He explains he didn't write a single line of code. That he prompted, designed his product, deployed it.
That AI does everything, you just need to "ask the right questions." Dozens of likes,
plenty of admiring comments, three consulting firms in his DMs asking to meet, a coffee booked with
Elon M... I'm getting carried away, but you get the dynamic.

And yet, you bump into the author every day. Sometimes he's a PM, sometimes a marketer, sometimes a designer.
And sometimes even a dev. The face changes, the script stays the same.
He gives off total mastery. But you know. You work with him, or with people like him, and you know that beneath
the polish, part of the machinery escapes him.
That he has no idea what an index on his database is. That his "scalability" rests on a free Vercel quota and three users a day.
That the day a real client calls, he'll have to pay someone like you to fix what Claude dropped there.

This isn't a critique of him. He played the game he was taught to play. He published at the right time, on the right network, in the right tone.
He turned a demo into a story, and a story into proof. That's exactly what's expected of him in 2026.

It's even what's expected of you, and you know it.

The strange part is, he doesn't look like he's faking. He looks like he believes what he's saying. He might be right to believe it.
Maybe all of this has become true. Maybe the old criteria (knowing how it works, debugging in prod under pressure,
explaining your stack to a junior) have just stopped mattering.

And if that's the case, we have a problem. Not them. Us.

## Us? What are we talking about?

Yes, us. You, me, all those who are asked to stay current, always on the front line, whatever the technology.
Us, the ones paid to know, who agreed to play a game where knowing is no longer enough. Where you have to know,
**AND** show it, **AND** anticipate what's next, **AND** climb the ladder, **AND** look relaxed through all of it.
It's like an arms race, except the weapons are called Claude Opus 52 or Node 99, and nobody asks if you can still read the manual.

I'm talking about that constant injunction to stay current, where you're told "if you're not on top of the latest version
of that tool, you're a _has been_." Where simply taking a week of vacation becomes a backlog to catch up on when you return.

I'm talking about that need to constantly communicate. Not just work, communicate. Say you tested this framework,
read that paper, used that model, and most of all what you thought about it. An opinion. Always an opinion. If you don't
have one, you're not following, and if you're not following, you're out of the loop.

I'm talking about that speech you hear too often: "Wait, you've got X years of experience and you're not a manager?
You're not making [_insert a salary I've never touched in my life_] a month?".
As if expertise was only ever a stepping stone, never a destination.

I'm talking about that constant expectation of the next wave. A bit like in chess, where you'd be expected to play at
master level, never at the regular player's level, even from players who can't calculate three moves ahead.
You have to be on top of things, have an opinion on the next innovation discovered during your tech watch, have tested it
yesterday evening between two episodes of the show everyone else is watching too.

And above all, what magicians call in [The Prestige](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Prestige_(film)),
the moment of magic: the instant when the illusion lands and the audience cries "WOW!", the moment when you have to
display all of it at once, without showing the seams, because showing the back of the curtain is an amateur's confession.
And being an amateur in your job, like in magic, when you're supposed to be a pro, is a no-go.

The problem is that all of this happens in parallel. Discovering the latest Claude model, posting about your learning,
climbing to manager, anticipating the next Claude model (!), and giving the impression that it all flows naturally.

It's mechanically impossible.

Nobody has the energy for it.

So either you crack, or you play the comedy.


## A comedy in six acts

### Act 1: The post that breaks through

The CTO of the moment just dropped a thread on X: he's telling you about Symfony 99, he details across 12 tweets all the
new features, the possibilities, why we now use this component like that, etc. In short: he tells you it's a game-changer,
and it breaks through: thousands of impressions, of likes, plenty of shares and follows.

But... Symfony 99 dropped in beta the day before yesterday, you know he couldn't have tested it seriously, he knows it too,
but that's not the point: others will talk about it too, but he'll have talked about it **FIRST**.

### Act 2: The hidden economy of posture

Our CTO might be a bit hasty, but mostly he wanted to share early and be noticed. That's commendable.

So let's talk about the AI influencer, who comes to explain that thanks to his agents, he automated his whole life,
and yours too. By the way, he gives you "the 5 prompts you absolutely must master."

And right at the end, he'll offer you his €99 course on how to succeed like him.

Because that's what we're talking about: a market where the influencer sells to those who fear falling behind.
Impostor syndrome isn't really a problem anymore, it has become a need to satisfy, a product to sell.

It's [The Emperor's New Clothes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Emperor%27s_New_Clothes),
they tell you they're dressing you in the finest garments, but really, you're naked.

### Act 3: AI as a catalyst

Yes, this comedy existed before AI arrived, but now it's nearly universal.

Before, faking knowledge required endurance: vocabulary, references, posture in meetings. You had to keep up the act,
which filtered out the actors. Today, anyone can open an AI assistant, paraphrase,
and pass for someone who masters a subject they're discovering.
These are [the Sunday experts](/en/developer-in-the-age-of-ai-between-vertigo-and-lucidity#sunday-experts).

The entry barrier for fake knowledge has collapsed. Nobody can tell the difference anymore between someone who dug for years
and someone who asked three questions the day before.

### Act 4: Is this only about devs?

No gatekeeping here: does your job involve some form of expertise? Then you're concerned.
You can be a doctor, a teacher, a lawyer, you can tell the same story, only the vocabulary changes.

Your story: getting life explained to you by a colleague, a peer, who didn't read it properly or doesn't have your level
of expertise on a subject you know better than they do.

The doctor who cites a meta-analysis they haven't read.

The lawyer who invokes a precedent discovered in the waiting room.

And the worst case: [the person outside your professional circle who can do it too](/en/when-everyone-thinks-they-can-do-your-job).

Tech didn't invent this comedy, it just has, now, an invisible prompter who can feed any line.
That prompter might be Claude for you, or any consumer AI agent for other professions.

### Act 5: What we actually reward

The problem with the CTO's post isn't the topic itself, it's the way it's done, it's symptomatic.
And as I was saying: in the other professions, the set and the actors change, but the comedy stays the same:
we reward performance in a field rather than its apprenticeship.

The influencer, the layman, and of course the random guy who shipped his SaaS in a weekend: all of them are the purest face
of this comedy, our modern Sganarelles. They aren't pretending. They've just learned to stop telling the difference
between doing and performing. They're in a system that rewards them for it.

### Act 6: You're not a spectator

And there it is. End of the show, and the curtain doesn't fall. The play loops, and everyone has ended up forgetting
that we were performing. At this stage, you have to admit you're no longer just a spectator. You know the codes.
You know when to smile at the right moment, nod in meetings, pretend that yes, you also read the paper.
The comedy has become the norm. If you refuse it, then you have to accept the price.

## The cost for those who refuse

Refusing the comedy has a price, it's not dramatic, you won't cry, but let's talk about it, since I know it well.

First, you collect a few labels: _not ambitious_, _he's stagnating_, _not lead material_.
You see promotions go by that could have been yours, friends becoming leads, others giving conference talks.
And honestly, without any sarcasm, I'm happy for them, it's just not my thing, and that's very personal.

Then there's the material ceiling. Fifteen years in the craft, and I earn less than most of my classmates from school,
I know this because we sometimes talk about it among ourselves, it's not a taboo. It's not because my companies pay badly,
it's that I never wanted to pay the price you had to pay to earn more.
The price was becoming someone else.

So I want to be honest, because at this stage, you might be wondering why I'm telling you all this.

I don't only have good reasons to refuse. I love my current working conditions. I love working in a small structure,
I love the rhythm, I've always preferred doing things well over selling them well, I've always preferred to evolve
in the shadows. This refusal is partly a choice... and partly a convenience.
But mostly, I think, a fear: of having to pretend every day, of not being able to keep up the illusion,
and of losing, over time, the very essence of my craft.

That's what refusal costs me, and that's what it earns me. Obviously you'll find bits of yourself in this, or nothing at all.
I'm just saying: if you recognize yourself, we're together, and if not, well, don't take me for a model.

Because this model has its own blind spots.


## The posture and its limits

With all my bla-bla, you could stop me and say:

> Yeah okay, you're explaining that you don't need to have ambition, but that's a bit easy, you have a privileged status
> that makes it comfortable.

And... well, let me explain.

### Ambition isn't a bad thing

When I talk about ambition, I'm talking about my situation as a tech senior who refuses the constant injunction of
"you must evolve", the one where it's tacitly forbidden to want to excel at your level, because your level is supposedly
reserved for people less experienced than you.

Ambition is a lever of emancipation for many people, it's even a way out for the working classes
and minorities. Just saying "don't have ambition" is a statement coming from people already settled.

In other words: you have a dream? It feels unreachable but you've got the drive? Then go for it, really go.

### Refusing is still a luxury

I said it, refusing to play the comedy has a price, in fact it's even a luxury. Mine rests on a few things: a permanent contract,
fifteen years of transferable skills, stable material conditions, no precarity at my back. So many
nets that let me say no without losing everything. The same refusal, voiced by someone who doesn't have these comforts,
doesn't carry the same weight, and may not even be allowed to be voiced. To act as if this asymmetry didn't exist
would be exactly the comedy of mastery applied to my own position: pretending not to know where I'm
speaking from, and looking the other way.

### Fear of failure as a philosophical posture

I said it earlier: there's a part of fear in my refusal. I don't want to have to pretend every day,
because I know I won't always hold the illusion. I can't always tell what's lucidity and what's
rationalization. When you disagree with a system, you'd like to believe it's an ethic, when sometimes
it's just self-protection.

I'll probably never know which of the two is more true in me, but I want to be honest with myself and with my limits.

### Work / life balance

Probably not going to make any friends here, but oh well.

There's a pattern that feels to me like the same comedy in a different costume. I observe, in my circle, men of
my generation claiming to share parenting duties 50/50, posting their outings with their kids and their
reflections on modern fatherhood. I don't know what their partners experience, I'm not in their place and I
won't pretend to be. I'm talking about the discourse, because that's what you can hear and observe, and because that
discourse is sometimes mine too. I'm part of it. And that discourse is a story of presence and balance, that sounds right
only if you don't dig.

At the office, he pretends to master what he's discovering. At home, he pretends to share what he barely skims.

If I can talk about it, it's because I've made choices of my own. Not out of virtue, out of necessity. It doesn't put me

However, refusing isn't enough, otherwise it's just vetoing and giving up. And that's where you need to know what you say yes to.


## Beyond refusal

What does developing mean? When I play the "explain your job to me like I'm five" game, I say:

> I produce simple solutions to problems that are complicated.

Because that's what my job is. Bringing a solution to a problem, solving issues, thinking about how to deploy it,
maintaining it, ensuring a good level of availability, and making sure the original need is met.

Since I started working, this craft has evolved. I've learned to use the tools at my disposal, I've picked up the
good practices, I keep doing my tech watch. I try new things.
But one thing I've learned: when something works, and works well, you keep it.
Not out of fear of change, but to guarantee stability. If the need evolves and the solution no longer
fits, then we see how to improve and possibly change the tool, but never out of hype.

I want to dig and deepen my knowledge of a practice, of a framework, of a technology, so I can think about it
on my own, without systematically asking Claude. I like knowing my solution so I can account for it when a
question or a problem arises, not having a superficial knowledge of it.

Collectively, we've chosen to value the manager over the expert. Yet there's nothing automatic about that, and it
isn't a fatality. Some companies, and among them the most powerful, have
[ladders](https://www.levels.fyi/?compare=Meta,Apple,Google) where a technical expert has the same status
as a manager. In France, apart from a few companies that imported the model, we talk about a "technical expert"
whose ceiling sits lower than the manager. That's exactly the symptom I'm describing.

AI isn't a tool like any other, I said it. Its power reveals mastery, or the lack of it. On a solid foundation,
it accelerates, but on emptiness, it composes in your place. That's why I want to pilot it from what I know,
as a conductor and not as an unprepared chorister.

---

I chose to refuse the chase for glory to do my craft well. Believing you can balance it all is lying to yourself.
Reality isn't a LinkedIn post.
